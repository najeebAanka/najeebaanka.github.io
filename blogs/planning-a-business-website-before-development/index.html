How to Plan a Business Website Before Development Starts

By Najeeb Anka

In this article

Start with one main outcome Map the journeys that matter Prepare content before polishing the layout Separate launch essentials from later improvements Match the platform to the actual requirements Agree on a launch checklist

A website project becomes easier to manage when the important decisions are made before the first screen is designed. A useful brief gives everyone a shared understanding of what to build, why it matters, and how to decide whether it is ready.

Start with one main outcome

Write down the primary job of the website in one sentence. For a consultancy, that might be helping qualified visitors request a consultation. For a property business, it might be helping buyers explore developments and submit an enquiry. Other goals can exist, but one clear priority helps settle design and content decisions.

Make the outcome observable. Instead of “make the site more professional,” describe the action a visitor should be able to complete and the information they need to feel confident doing it.

Map the journeys that matter

List your main audiences and the questions they bring. A first-time visitor may need to understand your services; a returning visitor may simply need your phone number. Build a short route for each important task.

  • Where does the visitor arrive?
  • What do they need to understand or compare?
  • What is the next useful action?
  • What happens after they take that action?

This exercise often reveals missing content, unnecessary pages, or a contact process that has not been agreed yet.

Prepare content before polishing the layout

Collect service descriptions, project examples, photographs, brand assets, and contact details early. Assign an owner to each piece of content and note anything that still needs approval. Real content exposes layout requirements that placeholder text hides.

For a project portfolio, decide what each case study should contain: the problem, your contribution, the features delivered, and evidence you can support. Avoid publishing performance figures or testimonials that have not been confirmed.

Separate launch essentials from later improvements

Create two lists: what must work for launch, and what can follow. The launch list should cover complete visitor journeys rather than disconnected features. “A visitor can browse services and send an enquiry” is more useful than “add a contact button.”

For every feature, record its content, dependencies, and acceptance criteria. A booking integration may depend on an external account. A contact form needs somewhere to send submissions. Agreeing on those details prevents a polished interface from hiding an incomplete process.

Match the platform to the actual requirements

A brochure or portfolio website can often be delivered as static pages. User accounts, private records, or custom submission handling may require a backend or a connected service. Discuss who will update the site, what they need to change, and how often.

Choose the approach after those requirements are clear. Document ownership of hosting, the domain, third-party accounts, and source files so handover is part of the plan.

Agree on a launch checklist

Before development starts, define how the finished website will be reviewed. Include mobile layouts, navigation, links, readable content, keyboard access, contact actions, and any integrations. Assign one person to consolidate feedback and someone to approve the final content.

A useful first brief: one primary goal, the intended audience, a page list, three key visitor journeys, a content owner, required integrations, and a clear launch checklist.

You do not need a lengthy document to begin. A short, specific brief gives the designer and developer enough direction to ask better questions and build a more useful first version.
