Write Stakeholder Updates That Help Decisions Happen

By Najeeb Anka

In this article

Lead with the current situation Report progress through observable results Make decisions explicit Separate issues from emerging risks Choose a format readers can scan Close the loop on earlier requests

A stakeholder update should make the state of a project understandable and show where someone needs to act. A long activity list can hide the important issue: the team may be waiting for a decision that only the reader can make.

Lead with the current situation

Open with a short assessment of the agreed outcome and upcoming milestone. Explain what is ready, what is uncertain, and whether the current plan still looks achievable. If you use a status colour, add the reason in words.

A useful opening might say that the main journey is ready for review, while the release date depends on approval of the final content. The reader can then interpret the detail in context.

Report progress through observable results

Describe what changed since the previous update. An accepted design, a reviewed implementation, or a completed handover is easier to assess than a list of meetings attended. Connect the progress to the milestone it supports.

Distinguish work that is complete from work that still needs approval. Something being available for review does not mean the review has happened.

Make decisions explicit

For every decision needed, state the question, relevant options, your recommendation if appropriate, and the consequence of waiting. Name the person who can decide and the date by which the answer matters.

For example: choosing between an existing integration and a custom workflow may affect both scope and timing. A focused comparison helps the owner make that choice without reading a technical conversation from the beginning.

Separate issues from emerging risks

Explain what has already happened and what might happen. A missing account that blocks today’s work is different from a supplier response that could arrive late next week. Both may need attention, but their actions can differ.

Keep the language factual. Include what the team is doing, who owns the response, and when the situation will be reviewed again.

Choose a format readers can scan

Use a predictable structure so important changes are easy to find. Keep supporting detail in a linked record or a short appendix when needed. Different audiences may need different depth, but they should receive a consistent account of the project.

  • Current status and next milestone.
  • Meaningful progress since the last update.
  • Risks, issues, and actions.
  • Decisions needed, with owners and dates.
  • Next steps before the following update.

Close the loop on earlier requests

Carry forward decisions until they are resolved or no longer relevant. Record the answer and explain any resulting change to scope or timing. If a decision is overdue, follow the agreed escalation route instead of repeatedly copying the same unresolved line.

Before sending: can the reader identify what changed, what needs attention, and what you need from them? If the answer is unclear, shorten the update and make the request more specific.
